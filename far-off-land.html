Written in 1959

A scholar’s notebook from 1959

When Seaich wrote The Far-Off Land, LSD and mescaline were legal in the United States and studied openly. He and a few University of Utah colleagues pooled ten dollars each and ordered their supply by mail from a New York laboratory. Sandoz, the manufacturer, sent written instructions, and the co-author of the leading pharmacology textbook offered advice.

Seaich was a musician, a registered pharmacist and a scholar of ancient religious texts. He timed and wrote down each session, then set the experience against myth, mysticism, poetry and the psychology of memory. His conclusion gives the book its title: the visions were not new inventions but a return to the “far-off land” of childhood and the unconscious.

All nine sessions

Session 1 of 9 · August 1959

The first session

Mescaline

The opening entry runs from mid-morning into the night. Nausea gives way to color, then to music: he notes a long stretch of “sheer bliss with music (Wagner)” before visitors break the spell.

Journal · August 1959
8:00City lights (in darkness) look like jewels. Very intense and colorful.

Session 2 of 9 · November 8, 1959

The threshold

LSD

A deliberately small first trial of LSD. At first he believes the drug is not working; by mid-afternoon he records music playing in his mind “much more palpable than usual daydreaming.”

Journal · November 8, 1959
4:30Poetry (T. S. Eliot, Four Quartets) seems to have much more intense meaning.

Session 3 of 9 · November 21, 1959

Testing an antidote

LSD

Seaich tests whether a vitamin, nicotinamide, can blunt the effects. It barely does. A walk at dusk ties the experience to childhood memory, the book’s central theme.

Journal · November 21, 1959
5:30Went for a walk in the dusk. The winter scene had a novel freshness about it. Seemed to be pervaded by a long-forgotten childhood sensation.

Session 4 of 9 · December 17, 1959

The first full mystical experience

LSD

The longest and most intense of the early sessions: kaleidoscopic visions, Mahler’s Third Symphony, and an evening by the Christmas-tree lights in which he recreated “the sensation of Christmas as I knew it in childhood.”

Journal · December 17, 1959
3:45Can hear crickets chirping — am back in a childhood scene — a summer evening — this most unusual!

Session 5 of 9 · January 3, 1960

A challenging experience, and recovery

LSD

Bliss with Mozart turns into emptiness and “utter zero-ness.” Then the mood lifts again. Seaich records the difficult hours as plainly as the good ones.

Journal · January 3, 1960
2:40World seems cold, strange, empty. Can’t make my inner self work this time.

Session 6 of 9 · January 23, 1960

The great childhood-memory session

Mescaline

After an hour of regret and nausea come color, flame-like visions and a sense of “creative energy, revitalization.” He ends the day in “a harmonious emotion of outpouring goodwill and happiness.”

Journal · January 23, 1960
1:30All splendid fire and heat; a divine force pulsating in vision beyond all description.

Session 7 of 9 · February 28, 1960

A threshold-state experiment

LSD

A small-dose experiment in mood. Listening to Tristan, he finds childhood recollections “trying to draw me back against my will into their sinister magic.”

Journal · February 28, 1960
5:00A strange world of otherness, neither euphoric nor dysphoric, ensued.

Session 8 of 9 · March 5, 1960

A lesson in set and setting

LSD

Telephone calls and interruptions cut across the peak of the session, and he records what was lost. The entry reads today as an early account of why setting matters.

Journal · March 5, 1960
4:00Light! Pure light! Just to sit and stare into translucent Being!

LSD

The author

John Eugene Seaich (1925–2006)

Born in Salt Lake City, Seaich earned a Ph.D. in Music from the University of Utah in 1958, studied in Germany as a Fulbright grantee, affiliated as a student with the University of Munich, worked as a registered pharmacist and later wrote on ancient religious texts. The Far-Off Land stayed in his library for decades. His grandson, Eric Hendrickson, found the typescript after his death and prepared this edition.

Questions readers ask

Short answers

All questions and answers

What is The Far-Off Land?

The Far-Off Land is a philosophical essay and field journal by the scholar John Eugene Seaich, written in Salt Lake City in 1959, when LSD and mescaline were legal and studied openly. It argues that psychedelic substances can open the deep, early layers of memory — the “far-off land” of the unconscious — and it records nine dated self-experiments made between August 1959 and October 1960.

Who was John Eugene Seaich?

John Eugene Seaich (1925–2006) studied at the University of Utah, where he earned a Ph.D. in Music in 1958. He was a Fulbright grantee at the University of Munich (1955–56), a registered pharmacist, and later a writer on ancient religious texts.

What does the Complete Annotated Edition add?

It presents Seaich’s 1959 text, presented in the restored edition, with an editor’s apparatus: contemporary-research commentary boxes, numbered chapter notes, a glossary, an annotated bibliography, a biographical index, a topical guide, a guide to research organizations, a documented record of the author’s training, a parallel chronology, and photographs of the typescript itself.

Is this book a guide to using psychedelics?

No. It is a historical and scholarly work. The self-experiments it records belong to the practices and legal context of 1959–1960 and must not be taken as guidance for present-day conduct. Nothing in it is medical advice, and the substances discussed remain controlled under the laws of most jurisdictions.

Why does a 1959 text matter to today’s psychedelic renaissance?

Seaich describes, decades before they were named or measured, themes that current research now studies: the persistence of early memory, the loosening of the ordinary self, the role of music and of “set and setting,” challenging experiences, and mystical-type states. The commentary shows where later findings — the default mode network, the REBUS model, mystical-experience research — echo his observations, and where they diverge.
