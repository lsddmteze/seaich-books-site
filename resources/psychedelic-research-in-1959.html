Psychedelic research in 1959: the world of the manuscript

When Seaich wrote The Far-Off Land, psychedelic research was legitimate, clinical, and young — and only a few writers were doing philosophy with it.

Updated November 3, 2026

The first era

  1. 1943 — Albert Hofmann discovers LSD’s psychoactivity in Basel. Sandoz supplies it to researchers for two decades.
  2. 1954 — Aldous Huxley publishes The Doors of Perception, his mescaline account and the decade’s famous first-person record.
  3. 1956–57 — Humphry Osmond coins “psychedelic” in a 1956 letter to Huxley and introduces it publicly in 1957.
  4. 1957 — R. C. Zaehner answers Huxley in Mysticism Sacred and Profane.
  5. Throughout the 1950s — clinicians in Saskatchewan, elsewhere in North America and in Europe study LSD and mescaline as models of psychosis, as aids to psychotherapy and as treatments for alcoholism. The paradigm is medical.

The gap

Clinical papers ask whether these compounds work. Huxley asks what they mean — once, literarily. Almost nobody in 1959 is asking, systematically and philosophically: what is this experience, what is its structure, and what follows from taking it seriously? That is the gap Seaich’s manuscript occupies — nine disciplined first-person sessions evaluated as philosophy, written before the 1960s made every position a battle line.

The long silence

Prohibition in the late 1960s ended the first era; the second — Johns Hopkins from 2000, today’s Phase 3 programs — is still largely clinical. The philosophical literature between Huxley and the present is thin. A 1959 philosophical manuscript is not a curiosity from that silence. It is one of the few things that was being said during it.
