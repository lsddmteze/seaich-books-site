Mormonism, the Dead Sea Scrolls, and the Nag Hammadi Texts

Early Christianity, Gnosticism, LDS Temple Traditions, and Ancient Religious Parallels

Did Joseph Smith teach what the ancient scrolls contain, a century before they were found? Joseph Smith died in 1844. The Nag Hammadi library came to light in 1945, the Dead Sea Scrolls in 1947. Yet these ancient Jewish and Christian writings speak of a premortal life of the soul, a Council in Heaven, a divine Mother, a “Bridal Chamber” where husband and wife are joined forever, graded heavens, and men and women who become like God.

What the book argues

Written in reply to a 1979 critique of Latter-day Saint use of the new discoveries, this study moves beyond “parallelomania” — collecting resemblances without showing a real historical connection. Seaich follows each idea along its historical path, from ancient Israel and Qumran through the New Testament to the Gnostic gospels, and asks which resemblances reveal a real connection. That is Seaich’s interpretation, developed in the chapters that follow. It is not a settled historical finding, and not an official statement of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints.

This is a work of comparative religion and Latter-day Saint interpretation, not a general introduction to the Dead Sea Scrolls or the Nag Hammadi library. Parallels are weighed one by one, with attention to where the evidence is strong, where it is suggestive, and where it is contested.

Questions the book answers

Short answers

Fifteen questions, answered briefly. The book argues each one in full.

What are the Dead Sea Scrolls?

The Dead Sea Scrolls are more than 900 Jewish manuscripts found between 1947 and 1956 in eleven caves near Qumran, by the Dead Sea. They date from roughly the third century B.C. to the first century A.D. They include the oldest surviving copies of most books of the Hebrew Bible and sectarian works such as the Community Rule, which many scholars associate with the Essenes.

What is the Nag Hammadi library?

The Nag Hammadi library is a collection of Coptic codices found in December 1945 near Nag Hammadi in Upper Egypt. It contains over fifty texts, many of them associated with Gnostic forms of Christianity. Among them are the Gospel of Thomas, the Gospel of Philip and the Gospel of Truth. The surviving copies date from the fourth century, but many of the texts were composed earlier in Greek.

Do the Dead Sea Scrolls or the Nag Hammadi texts mention Mormonism or Joseph Smith?

No. Neither collection mentions Joseph Smith or Latter-day Saint teachings by name, and neither is a record of modern Mormonism. Seaich’s argument concerns themes in these ancient texts, such as premortal life, temple ritual and sacred marriage, and whether those themes are historically connected to the Primitive Church.

Could Joseph Smith have read these texts?

Not these manuscripts. Joseph Smith died in 1844, and the Nag Hammadi and Qumran discoveries were made in 1945 and 1947. As the Foreword notes, however, some related ideas were available in his time through the Bible and earlier publications. For example, Richard Laurence’s English translation of 1 Enoch appeared in 1821. A careful comparison has to take both facts into account.

Did early Christians believe in a premortal existence of the soul?

Some did. Origen in the third century taught the pre-existence of souls, and the idea appears in Jewish sources such as the Wisdom of Solomon and the Talmud. Origenist teaching on pre-existence was later condemned in the sixth century. Seaich argues that the belief was more widespread in the earliest Church than later orthodoxy acknowledged.

Did ancient Israelites believe God had a wife?

Some evidence suggests that some Israelites did. Eighth-century B.C. inscriptions from Kuntillet ʿAjrud and Khirbet el-Qom bless people “by Yahweh and his Asherah.” Scholars debate whether “asherah” there means the goddess herself or a cult object. Seaich, drawing on Raphael Patai’s The Hebrew Goddess, argues for a divine Father, Mother and Son in early Israel.

What is the Bridal Chamber in the Gospel of Philip?

The Gospel of Philip names five sacraments: baptism, chrism, Eucharist, redemption and the Bridal Chamber, which it calls the highest, the “Holy of Holies.” Scholars disagree about whether the rite was purely spiritual or also involved earthly marriage. Seaich argues that it united husband and wife on the model of a heavenly union.

Does the Gospel of Philip say Jesus was married to Mary Magdalene?

Not explicitly. The text calls Mary Magdalene Jesus’ “companion” (Greek koinōnos) and says he loved her more than the other disciples and often kissed her. The word that would say where he kissed her is lost in a damaged part of the manuscript. Seaich reads the passages as evidence of marriage; many scholars read them symbolically.

What were the “embracing cherubim” of the Temple?

The Babylonian Talmud (Yoma 54a–b) reports that the cherubim in the Second Temple were shown to pilgrims “intertwined with each other.” Raphael Patai interpreted this as an image of a male and female in embrace. Seaich connects it with the Bridal Chamber and with the teaching that “he who does not marry diminishes the image of God.”

What is “parallelomania”?

“Parallelomania” is a term made popular by Samuel Sandmel in his 1961 presidential address to the Society of Biblical Literature, published in 1962. It describes collecting resemblances between texts without showing a real historical connection between them. Seaich accepts the warning and argues that only parallels with a demonstrable historical link count as evidence.

Did early Christians teach three degrees of glory?

Papias and Irenaeus, both of the second century, connected the “hundredfold, sixtyfold and thirtyfold” of Matthew 13:8 with three heavenly dwelling places: heaven, Paradise and the City. Paul also contrasts celestial and terrestrial glories (1 Cor. 15:40–41). Seaich compares these passages with the three degrees of glory in Doctrine and Covenants 76.

What does “Man of Holiness” mean?

Moses 6:57 gives “Man of Holiness” as a name of God and “Son of Man” as a name of his Son. Seaich compares this with Gnostic texts that call the highest God “Man” or “Perfect Man,” and with scholars who suggest that “Son of Man” carried similar overtones in Jesus’ day.

What does this annotated edition add?

It adds 13 chapters with descriptive titles and editor’s summaries, notes that correct citations and flag contested readings, modern equivalents for Dead Sea Scroll and Nag Hammadi references, Questions This Book Explores, Parallels at a Glance, this FAQ, a glossary, bibliographies of ancient and modern sources, and a linked index.

Who was Eugene Seaich?

Eugene Seaich (1925–2006) was a Utah scholar and author who wrote on the ancient roots of Latter-day Saint belief. He earned five degrees, including a Ph.D. in music from the University of Utah and a pharmacy degree, and spent a Fulbright year in Munich (1955–56). His other books include Ancient Texts and Mormonism and Understanding Mormonism.

What prompted this book?

Seaich wrote it as a reply to Melaine Layton’s booklet The Truth About the Dead Sea Scrolls and Nag Hammadi Writings in Reference to Mormonism (1979). Layton had challenged the popular lectures of Einar Erickson, which compared Latter-day Saint teachings with the new discoveries. Seaich argued that her critique attacked Erickson rather than the Latter-day Saint position, and set out what he saw as the real historical connections.

Why a new annotated edition?

This edition is designed to help readers not only encounter Seaich’s arguments but examine them. A new introduction covers the scholarship since Seaich wrote, including what later discoveries — from Kuntillet ʿAjrud’s “Yahweh and his Asherah” to the full publication of the Dead Sea Scrolls — have added. Editorial notes, source references and a parallels table place his interpretation in context and make the work easier to consult, discuss and evaluate.

Who it is for

For Latter-day Saints, students of the Dead Sea Scrolls and Gnosticism, and anyone curious about how diverse early Christianity really was.

About the author

Eugene Seaich (1925–2006) earned five degrees, including a Ph.D. in Music from the University of Utah. He was a Fulbright grantee in Germany from October 1955 to July 1956 and taught at the University of Utah. He studied the Dead Sea Scrolls, the Nag Hammadi library, rabbinic literature and the early Church Fathers. His documented biography.
