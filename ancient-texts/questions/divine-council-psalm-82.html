Psalm 82: What Did God Mean by "Ye Are Gods"?

By Eric Hendrickson · Based on the research of Eugene Seaich, Ph.D. · Updated September 26, 2026 · 10-minute read

Quick answer

Psalm 82 pictures God standing “in the assembly of El” and judging “among the gods.” God condemns the assembly members for injustice and declares, “Ye are gods… but ye shall die like men.” Interpreters identify the “gods” as human judges, Israel at Sinai, or members of a heavenly divine council. Most modern critical scholars favor the divine council reading.

The Psalm

Psalm 82 is a short “Psalm of Asaph,” only eight verses long. Psalm 82 is among the most discussed passages in the Hebrew Bible:

1 God standeth in the congregation of the mighty; he judgeth among the gods.
2 How long will ye judge unjustly, and accept the persons of the wicked?
3 Defend the poor and fatherless: do justice to the afflicted and needy…
6 I have said, Ye are gods; and all of you are children of the most High.
7 But ye shall die like men, and fall like one of the princes.
8 Arise, O God, judge the earth: for thou shalt inherit all nations.Psalm 82:1–3, 6–8, King James Version

The scene is a courtroom. God rises in an assembly, accuses its members of failing the poor and the fatherless, and pronounces sentence: those called "gods" and "children of the Most High" will die like mortals.

What the Hebrew Says

The English “God” and “gods” in verse 1 translate the same Hebrew word, ʾĕlōhîm. The singular and plural grammar distinguishes the two senses: the first takes a singular verb (“stands”), and the second follows “in the midst of,” so the second occurrence must refer to more than one.

VerseHebrewLiteral meaning
82:1aאֱלֹהִים נִצָּב בַּעֲדַת־אֵלʾĕlōhîm niṣṣāb baʿădat-ʾēl"God stands in the assembly of El"
82:1bבְּקֶרֶב אֱלֹהִים יִשְׁפֹּטbĕqereb ʾĕlōhîm yišpōṭ"in the midst of the gods he judges"
82:6אֱלֹהִים אַתֶּם וּבְנֵי עֶלְיוֹן כֻּלְּכֶםʾĕlōhîm ʾattem ûbĕnê ʿelyôn kullĕkem"You are gods, and sons of the Most High, all of you"

The phrase "assembly of El" (ʿădat-ʾēl) closely resembles language in the Canaanite texts from Ugarit (c. 1300 BCE), which describe an "assembly of the gods" gathered around the high god El. For many scholars, the Ugaritic parallel is the strongest evidence that Psalm 82 draws on the ancient Near Eastern idea of a divine council. Claim Level 3

Three Ancient Interpretations

1. Human judges

The "gods" are Israel's rulers or judges, called ʾĕlōhîm because they exercised God's authority (compare Exodus 21:6 and 22:8–9, where some translations render ʾĕlōhîm as "judges"). Many Jewish and Protestant commentators, including Calvin, accepted the human-judges view.

2. Israel at Sinai

A rabbinic reading (Babylonian Talmud, Avodah Zarah 5a) applies "Ye are gods" to Israel receiving the Torah. Israel would have been immortal, but the sin of the golden calf brought the sentence "ye shall die like men."

3. The divine council

The “gods” are heavenly beings who govern the nations under the Most High. The heavenly beings failed in their charge and are sentenced to mortality. The divine-council interpretation is the majority view in modern critical scholarship. Claim Level 3

An early witness to the third view comes from the Dead Sea Scrolls. The scroll 11QMelchizedek (11Q13) applies Psalm 82:1 to Melchizedek as a heavenly figure who stands in the divine assembly and executes judgment. At least some Jews of the Second Temple period read the psalm as describing heavenly beings. Claim Level 3

The NIV puts "gods" in quotation marks, signaling hesitation. The ESV, NRSV, NLT and the Jewish Publication Society translation all render the scene as a divine assembly, which shows how widely the council reading is now accepted.

The Link to Deuteronomy 32:8

Psalm 82 reads like the sequel to Deuteronomy 32:8–9. In Deuteronomy, as the Dead Sea Scroll manuscript reads the verse, the Most High divides the nations “according to the number of the sons of God.” In Psalm 82, those same "sons of the Most High" are judged for ruling the nations unjustly. The last verse then uses the same key word, inherit (nāḥal): "Arise, O God, judge the earth: for thou shalt inherit all nations."

Read together, the two passages tell one story. God assigned the nations to his council, the council failed, and God takes the nations back as his own inheritance. Claim Level 3

Jesus and John 10:34

Psalm 82 is quoted in the New Testament. Accused of blasphemy "because that thou, being a man, makest thyself God," Jesus replied:

"Is it not written in your law, I said, Ye are gods? If he called them gods, unto whom the word of God came, and the scripture cannot be broken; say ye of him, whom the Father hath sanctified, and sent into the world, Thou blasphemest; because I said, I am the Son of God?"John 10:34–36, King James Version

Jesus argues from the lesser to the greater. If Scripture calls those who received God’s word “gods,” the scriptural title makes it impossible to call the one sent by the Father blasphemous merely for calling himself God’s Son. Interpreters disagree about whom Jesus meant by "them… unto whom the word of God came." Some say Israel at Sinai, some say Israel's judges, and some say the divine council. Claim Level 2

Early Christians and Becoming "Gods"

Early Christian writers often read Psalm 82:6 as a promise about human destiny. The teaching of human deification came to be called theosis:

  • Justin Martyr (Dialogue with Trypho 124, c. 160 CE) used the psalm to argue that humans were made to become like God and were "deemed worthy of becoming gods."
  • Irenaeus of Lyon (Against Heresies 4.38.4, c. 180 CE) cited "I have said, Ye are gods" and wrote that we "have not been made gods from the beginning, but at first merely men, then at length gods."
  • Athanasius (4th century) summed up the idea: God "became man so that we might become god."

Theosis remains a central teaching of Eastern Orthodox Christianity today.

What Eugene Seaich Concluded

Eugene Seaich (Ph.D., University of Utah; Fulbright scholar; author of A Great Mystery, Gorgias Press) saw Psalm 82 as one of the clearest windows into the theology of ancient Israel. In Volume 2 of Ancient Texts and Mormonism, he argues:

  • Psalm 82 preserves an older belief in a council of divine beings presided over by the supreme God. Claim Level 3
  • The "sons of the Most High" were understood as true members of God's heavenly family, not merely symbolic titles for human judges. Claim Level 2
  • The early Christian reading of verse 6, that humans may become “gods,” continued the ancient idea of divine kinship. Claim Level 2
  • The divine council and the promise of deification resemble the Restoration’s teaching of a Council in Heaven and of exaltation. Claim Level 4

Latter-day Saint scripture speaks of "the Council of the Eternal God of all other gods" (Doctrine and Covenants 121:32) and describes the exalted as "gods, even the sons of God" (D&C 76:58). Joseph Smith cited "Ye are gods" in his sermons of 1844. Claim Level 1

"A similar arrangement is depicted in Psalm 82, where Elohim still presides over the ʿadat ʾEl (vs. 1), made up once again of the 'sons of God' (bene ʿElyon), i.e. the lesser gods (ʾelohim, vs. 6), who rule over the various nations."Eugene Seaich, Ancient Texts and Mormonism, Vol. 2: The Mormon Concept of Deity

What Skeptics Say

  • The human-judges reading is old and defensible. The psalm's accusations (partiality, neglect of the poor) sound like charges against earthly rulers, and ʾĕlōhîm may mean "judges" elsewhere in the Torah.
  • A council does not mean equal gods. Many scholars who accept the council reading, including Michael Heiser, stress that the council members are created beings, far below Israel’s God, and are condemned in Psalm 82.
  • The Church Fathers meant something different. Justin, Irenaeus and Athanasius taught that humans share in God's life by grace. The Church Fathers did not teach that humans become gods of the same kind as God. Latter-day Saint and Orthodox understandings of "becoming gods" differ in important ways.
  • Resemblance does not equal transmission. A parallel between Psalm 82 and Restoration teaching does not by itself prove a historical connection. “Resemblance does not equal transmission” is the governing rule of the entire series.

Questions

Who are the "gods" in Psalm 82?

Interpreters give three main answers: human judges or rulers of Israel; Israel at Mount Sinai (a rabbinic reading); or members of God's heavenly divine council. Most modern critical scholars favor the divine council reading, because verse 1 describes God standing in "the assembly of El," a phrase with close parallels in ancient Canaanite texts.

What does "Ye are gods" mean in Psalm 82:6?

In Psalm 82:6 God addresses the members of the assembly: "I have said, Ye are gods; and all of you are children of the most High." Verse 7 then announces that the assembly members will “die like men” because the assembly members judged unjustly. The meaning depends on whether the addressees are human rulers, Israel, or divine beings.

Why did Jesus quote Psalm 82 in John 10:34?

When accused of blasphemy for claiming to be the Son of God, Jesus answered, "Is it not written in your law, I said, Ye are gods?" He argued that if Scripture calls those who received God's word "gods," the scriptural wording prevents the title “God’s Son” from being blasphemous when used by the one the Father sanctified and sent. Scholars debate exactly whom Jesus understood the "gods" to be.

How do Latter-day Saints interpret Psalm 82?

Latter-day Saints often read Psalm 82 as evidence for a council of divine beings and for humanity's divine potential. Joseph Smith cited Psalm 82 in 1844, and Doctrine and Covenants 76:58 describes the exalted as "gods, even the sons of God." Early Church Fathers such as Irenaeus also used Psalm 82:6 to teach deification (theosis).
